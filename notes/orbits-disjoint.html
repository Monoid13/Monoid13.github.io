Orbits are disjoint

Disjoint. Two sets are disjoint if they have no element in common, that is, \(A\cap B=\varnothing\).

Proposition. Let \(G\) act on \(X\). Any two orbits \(Gx\) and \(Gy\) are either equal or disjoint.

Proof. Suppose \(Gx\) and \(Gy\) are not disjoint, so some \(z\) lies in both: \(z=gx=hy\) for some \(g,h\in G\). Then \(x=g^{-1}hy\).(\(\subseteq\)) For any \(k\in G\), \(kx=(kg^{-1}h)y\in Gy\). So \(Gx\subseteq Gy\).(\(\supseteq\)) By the same argument with \(x\) and \(y\) swapped, \(Gy\subseteq Gx\).So \(Gx=Gy\). Hence two orbits that share an element are equal, and otherwise they are disjoint. \(\blacksquare\)

Consequence. Every \(x\) lies in its own orbit, since \(x=ex\in Gx\). So the orbits partition \(X\).
